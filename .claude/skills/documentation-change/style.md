# Style of a documentation page

Each rule comes with what it protects the reader from. Each "Before" shows a tic as a DrinkIt page once wrote
it, each "After" a way to write it instead. The tics come from text generated by language models, and Wikipedia's
[Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) lists more of them.

## Sentences

- **One idea per sentence, 25 words at most.** A reader holds one idea at a time. A sentence with two ideas
  becomes two sentences.
- **Active voice.** It names who acts: "Liquibase runs the files", not "the files are run".
- **Present tense.** The page describes what the project does now. "Will" belongs to a plan, and a plan belongs
  in an issue.
- **"You" for the reader, "we" for the project.** "You add a file to `ddl/`", "we commit the jOOQ classes".
- **A paragraph has one subject**, stated in its first sentence, in five sentences at most.

## Words

- **One concept, one word.** Use the word of the code and keep it to the end. Swapping synonyms for variety
  makes the reader wonder whether two things are meant.
  - Before: a tech starter, then a "Tech-Starter", a "starter", a "technical building block".
  - After: a tech starter, every time, defined once.
- **Define a term once, where it first appears**, in half a sentence: "a port (an interface the domain defines
  and an adapter implements)".
- **A fact that ages carries its version or date.** "Measured on PostgreSQL 18.6", "Konture 1.0.0, published on
  2026-09-23". The next reader can tell whether it still holds.
- **No inflated words.** "Essential", "crucial", "powerful", "robust", "seamless", "elegant", "leverage",
  "ensure" add weight without information. Say what the thing does.
  - Before: "Liquibase is an essential tool for tracking, managing, and applying database schema changes."
  - After: "Liquibase applies the schema changes and records which ones each database has received."
- **"Is" rather than "serves as" or "acts as".**
- **No "simply", "just", "easy" or "please".** What is easy for the writer may not be for the reader.

## Headings

- **Sentence case**: "Writing safe migrations", not "Writing Safe Migrations".
- **Say what the section holds.** A reader skims the headings to find their section.
  - Before: "Why we choose a **Monorepo**?"
  - After: "Why one repository"
- **One emoji at most, at the start of a `##` heading**, chosen for what the section holds, such as
  "## 🐘 Why Gradle" or "## 🔒 Trusting what the build downloads". It brings a little colour to a page of black
  text and helps the eye find a section. Keep it light: headings below `##` carry none, nor do the text, the
  lists and the tables. The site leaves the emoji out of the anchor, so adding one breaks no link.
  - Before: "## 1️⃣ Principles", "### 📂 Three Folders", "### Why Locks Matter 🔐"
  - After: "## 🧭 Principles", "### Three folders", "### Why locks matter"
- **No number, no link, no bold, no `{#anchor}`** in a heading, and no level skipped. A custom anchor shows as
  text on GitHub and in editor previews.

## Links

- **The link text names the target**, such as "ADR 0002 records why" with "ADR 0002" as the link, never "click
  here" or "here".
  - Before: "More details in some Medium articles [like here](https://medium.com/...)"
  - After: the article in "Sources", under its title.
- **Link a target at its first mention in each section**, since a reader often lands on one section from the
  outline. Never link it twice in the same section.
- **Link rather than repeat.** A command lives in `AGENTS.md` or a skill, a reason in an ADR, a rule in the file
  that checks it. Two copies drift apart.
- **No bare URL.** Between pages, use the relative path with its `.md` extension, which works on GitHub and on
  the site. VitePress also takes a path without it, as older pages write it: leave those as they are. A link to a
  generated page under `resources/` resolves once `./gradlew kspKotlin` has written it.

## Lists, tables and emphasis

- **Numbered list for steps, bullets for the rest.** A list item that needs a reason becomes a sentence.
- **A table from three columns.** Two columns read better as a list of labelled items.
- **Bold for a term at its definition**, and for the label of a list whose every item is a labelled paragraph,
  as in this file. Nowhere else.
  - Before: "Enable teams to **focus on business value** while ensuring quality, consistency, and maintainability."
  - After: "Feature code calls a starter and never deals with the technology behind it."
- **No checkmark or warning lists.**
  - Before: "✅ **Immediate visibility of change impact**", "⚠️ **Change coordination required**"
  - After: a section "What it costs" with one sentence per cost.
- **No horizontal rule between sections.** Headings already separate them.

## Punctuation

- **No em or en dash as an aside.** Use a full stop, a colon or parentheses. Replacing every dash with a comma
  only moves the tic.
  - Before: "Any finding fails the build — `failOnSeverity` is tightened from detekt's default."
  - After: "Any finding fails the build: `failOnSeverity` is stricter than detekt's default."
- **No semicolon in prose.** Two sentences read better.
- **No exclamation mark, no curly quotes.**

## Generated-text patterns

- **"Not X but Y"**, "it's not just X, it's Y", "rather than merely". State Y.
- **Groups of three by reflex**: "robust, standardized, and maintainable applications". Keep the one that is
  true and checkable.
- **A trailing "-ing" phrase** that adds a vague benefit: "…, ensuring logical and robust hierarchies". Cut it,
  or make it a sentence with a fact.
- **Inflated significance**: "plays a key role", "stands as a testament", "a cornerstone of".
- **Sales sections**: "Key benefits", "Why it matters", a closing "Challenges" or "Future outlook". Benefits
  belong to the reasons of each rule, costs to a section that names them.
- **Summary closers**: "In summary", "Overall", "In short". The first paragraph already holds the conclusion.
- **Empty transitions**: "It is worth noting that", "It is important to", "Let's dive into".
- **Vague attribution**: "experts agree", "it is widely considered". Name the source, and list it in "Sources".
- **The lecture**: teaching the reader what any engineer knows, the history of a pattern, a definition of REST or
  of a transaction. One sentence and a source are enough, the rest of the page is about DrinkIt.
  - Before: "Alistair Cockburn described the pattern in 2005, with this intent: [...] The hexagon is the business
    code of the application. Everything around it is an actor, on one of two sides:"
  - After: "The business code, the hexagon, talks to the outside world through ports only."
- **The tour of the code**: each setting, class or option in turn, with what it does. The reader came for the shape
  and the reasons, and the code holds the rest.
  - Before: five bullets, "Interfaces only", "No default body", "One interface per tag", "Validation from the
    schema", "Domain types for identifiers", each with a paragraph on the setting behind it.
  - After: "A controller implements the generated interface, so a forgotten operation fails the compilation, and a
    request that breaks the schema gets a 400 before it reaches the controller."
- **The count that announces a list**: "for four reasons", "three steps", "these six lists". A list grows and
  shrinks, and its count then goes wrong or takes an edit of its own. The list already shows how many items it holds.
  - Before: "We chose it over code first for four reasons:"
  - After: "We chose it over code first:"

## Drafting traces

The page shows nothing of how it was written:

- no placeholder, "TODO" or empty section
- no sentence about the page itself, such as "This section explains…"
- no restated instruction, no "I hope this helps", no knowledge-cutoff disclaimer
- no stub left in the sidebar: a page that has only its title is written or removed from the sidebar
